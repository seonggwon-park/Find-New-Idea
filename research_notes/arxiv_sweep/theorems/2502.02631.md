- Authors: Zechun Liu, Changsheng Zhao, ... Vikas Chandra (Meta). arXiv v1 2025-02-04, text read = latest version (updated 2025-10-13). Comment: "NeurIPS 2025".
- Full text: scratchpad/papers/2502.02631/2502.02631.txt (70k chars). Grep for theorem/lemma/proof/proposition: **zero hits**. The paper proves nothing. Every claim below is empirical (figures) or an explicitly labeled hypothesis.

## Q1. The 2-vs-3-bit learning transition

**Status: empirical observation + explicitly labeled hypothesis. Not a theorem.**

- Abstract: "Our findings reveal a notable learning transition between 2 and 3 bits: For 3-bits and above, the fine-tuned models stay close to their original pre-trained distributions, whereas for learning 2-bit networks or below, the representations change drastically."
- Sec. 2.2, item 3 (exact wording): "QAT behavior transition between bit-widths: Networks quantized to 3-bit/4-bit recover near full-precision accuracy after finetuning, while binary, ternary, and 2-bit saturate before achieving full accuracy. **We hypothesize** that QAT acts as 'compensation' for bit-widths above 2-bit, adjusting weights within adjacent quantization levels, and as 'reconstruction' below 2-bit, where weights adapt beyond nearby grids to form new representations. This is supported by weight change analysis in Figure 4, showing smaller adjustments in 3-bit/4-bit (10-20%) and larger shifts in lower-bit quantization (~40%), indicating substantial value reconstruction."
- Finding-2 (boxed): "For 3- and 4-bit weights, finetuning adjusts within a nearby grid to mitigate accuracy loss, and requires less finetuning tokens. In contrast, binary and ternary weights break the grid, creating new semantic representations to maintain performance, requiring longer finetuning."
- Evidence, in full:
  1. Figure 3 (accuracy vs fine-tune tokens 3B/10B/30B/100B, 7 models: MobileLLM 125M–1.5B, LLaMA-3 1B/3B): 3/4-bit reach near-FP accuracy; 1/1.58/2-bit saturate below FP.
  2. Figure 4: one scalar per bit-width, ||W_finetune − W_init||_1 / ||W_init||_1. Values read from text only: 3/4-bit "10-20%", ≤2-bit "~40%". Model size and token count for Fig. 4 are not stated.
- Critical notes (mine):
  - "Representations change drastically" in the abstract is measured **only in weight space** (relative L1 of weights). The paper does not measure activations, hidden states, or output distributions.
  - **Confound.** Main settings (Sec. 5.1): 1/1.58/2-bit run "120,000 iterations with initial learning rate of 2 × 10^-5"; 3/4-bit run "40,000 iterations with initial learning rate of 1 × 10^-5". So 3× more steps at 2× the learning rate would increase weight drift by itself. If Fig. 4 uses these settings (not stated), the 40% vs 10–20% gap does not isolate bit-width. The quantizer also changes across the boundary (SEQ for 1.58/2-bit, LSQ for 3/4-bit; Eq. 5).
  - "Beyond nearby grids" is not shown. A relative-L1 change of 40% of mean|W| is not the same as moving more than one grid step Δ. With SEQ 2-bit, Δ = 2α/k = α/2 and α initialised at max|W|, so 0.4·mean|W| is still well below Δ unless the learned α shrinks a lot. That is my inference: the paper reports neither α trajectories nor cell-crossing rates.
  - The "transition" is located only on the discrete grid {1, 1.58, 2, 3, 4}. No 2.5-bit or mixed point is tested, so "sharp" means "between two adjacent tested settings".

## Q1b. The "~10% QAT budget" claim

- Sec. 2.1, Eq. (1): B*_FPT, B*_QAT = argmin_{B_FPT + B_QAT = B_train} L(B_FPT, B_QAT | N, P).
- Setup: "we evaluate various allocation ratios of B_FPT and B_QAT on **MobileLLM-125M** across four bit-widths (1.58-bit, 2-bit, 3-bit, and 4-bit)". App. A.5: B_train = **100B tokens**. The ratio is swept over [0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 1]. FP phase: lr 2.5e-3, weight decay 0.1, batch 16, 8×8 GPUs. QAT phase: lr 1e-4, no weight decay, batch 8. Each phase runs its own full LR schedule.
- Result text: "accuracy peaks at ∼90% FPT allocation **for almost every** bit-width choice, then decline sharply when FPT exceeds 90%."
- Finding-1 (boxed, exact): "QAT finetuning consistently surpasses both PTQ with B_FPT = B_train and QAT from scratch with B_QAT = B_train. Optimal performance is **nearly achieved** by dedicating the majority of the training budget to full precision (FP) training and **approximately 10%** to QAT."
- Scope: one model (125M), one total budget (100B tokens, about 800 tokens/param), 1-bit not included. Results are shown in Figure 2 only, with no numeric table. With the grid points 0.8 / 0.9 / 0.95, the optimum is resolved only to a QAT share of roughly 5–20%. "~10%" means 10% of the **total** budget (FP + QAT), not 10% of the pretraining tokens.
- **Contradicted by a follow-up.** Dremov et al., "Compute-Optimal Quantization-Aware Training", arXiv 2509.22935 (Apple/EPFL, ICLR 2026), uses ParetoQ's own quantizers and finds that "contrary to previous findings, the loss-optimal ratio of QAT to FP training increases with the total amount of compute", predicted by tokens-per-parameter-byte. Quoted: "This challenges the previous conclusion that 10% is universally optimal". Also quoted: "for 1-bit QAT, the same loss can be achieved with just around 50% of compute if the optimal QAT fraction is used". Its intro: "Liu et al. (2025) showed that spending 10% of the training steps on QAT is optimal for some setups, the authors did not explore how this proportion varies across different training lengths and model sizes."

## Q1c. Token requirements per bit-width

- Figure 3 caption: "Fine-tuning typically saturates at **10B tokens for 3-bit and 4-bit**, and at **30B tokens for 1-bit, 1.58-bit, and 2-bit**. Fine-tuning consistently outperforms training from scratch in both accuracy and token efficiency across all bit configurations."
- Sec. 2.2 item 2: "3-bit and 4-bit reach near full precision accuracy after 10B tokens, while lower-bit quantization saturates around 30B tokens."
- The token grid is {3B, 10B, 30B, 100B}, so "saturates at 30B" means it is flat between 30B and 100B, a factor-of-3 resolution. These are **absolute** token counts applied to models from 125M to 3B; they are not a fraction of pretraining.
- Consistency check (my arithmetic): 16 GPUs × batch 8 × 120k iterations = 15.36M sequences, about 31.5B tokens if the sequence length is 2048. 40k iterations gives about 10.5B tokens. That matches 30B/10B. The training sequence length is not stated; 2048 appears only in the latency setup.

## Other exact numbers (for context)
- Sec. 4.1: "a 2-bit MobileLLM-1B model achieves 1.8 points higher accuracy than a 4-bit MobileLLM-600M model, with even smaller model sizes." Effective size = (#weights×weight-bits + #embedding-weights×embedding-bits)/8. Embeddings and output layer are **not quantized** in the main results (Sec. 5.1).
- Contribution bullet: the 1.58-bit ParetoQ LLaMA-3 8B "reduces the performance gap to full precision by relatively 37.8% compared to the 1-bit Era's LLaMA-3 8B model ... while using only 30% of the training tokens."
- App. A.4 (GPU, vs Machete 4-bit): for 1B/3B/8B shapes "the performance speed-up from reducing weight precision from 4-bit to 2-bit is minimal ... 4-bit quantization achieves a more favorable speed-accuracy trade-off in these settings." At 16384×16384 the 2-bit kernel is 4.14× FP16 and 1.24× Machete-4bit. The headline "2-bit faster at same accuracy" (Sec. 4.3, Fig. 7c) is a **CPU** (Apple M1) result.

## Paper's own limitations / future work
There is no Limitations section. Grep for "limitation" matches only Sec. 4.2 "memory limitations". The caveats it does state:
- Contributions: "widespread adoption will require community-wide efforts, such as INT2 support in NVIDIA tensor cores, to unlock the full benefits of 2-bit quantization."
- Sec. 4.2: ternary storage "is effective only when sparsity exceeds 90%, due to high indexing costs"; some approaches "store ternary values as 2-bit signed integers, negating the expected storage benefits."
- App. A.4: on GPU, 2-bit gives minimal speed-up for small shapes because of conversion overhead.
- The paper explicitly avoids fitting a functional law: "Rather than fitting hypothetical scaling laws for quantization, ParetoQ demonstrate[s] its robustness by yielding state-of-the-art (SOTA) models". The "scaling laws" in the title are Pareto plots, not fitted equations.
- Weight-only quantization; activations, KV cache, embeddings and LM head stay in full precision.
