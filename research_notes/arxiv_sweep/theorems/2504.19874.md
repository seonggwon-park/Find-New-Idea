## 0. What the paper proves vs. claims (cold reading)

The paper has three theorems and four lemmas. All of them are about **MSE or inner-product distortion of a single unit-norm vector** (Eq. (1)–(2)); none is about attention, softmax, token-level or task-level error.

- **Proved** (with assumptions): Theorem 1 (MSE upper bound of TurboQuant_mse, exact identity D_mse = d·C(f_X,b) plus a numerically/asymptotically evaluated constant), Theorem 2 (unbiasedness + variance of TurboQuant_prod, conditional on Lemma 4), Theorem 3 (lower bound for *any* randomized quantizer via Yao + Shannon lower bound), Lemma 1 (Beta marginal), Lemma 3 (SLB on the sphere, with a Stirling step).
- **Heuristic, not used in any proof**: "near-independence" of rotated coordinates. The proof of Theorem 1 uses only linearity of expectation and identical marginals (Lemma 1); independence is invoked only to *motivate* per-coordinate scalar quantization.
- **Asymptotic, presented as a bound**: the constant √3π/2 ≈ 2.72 comes from the Panter–Dite high-resolution formula, applied by the paper "for larger bit-widths b > 4"; for b = 1..4 the paper substitutes numerically solved Gaussian Lloyd–Max costs. The statement "for any b ≥ 0" in Theorem 1 is therefore a mix of numerical values (b ≤ 4) and a high-rate approximation (b > 4), not a single rigorous inequality. Also, the b ≤ 4 numbers are for the Gaussian N(0,1/d) limit, not the exact Beta marginal ("For moderate values of d, f_X → N(0, 1/d)").
- **Claimed empirically** (Section 4): LongBench(-E) averages on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Ministral-7B-Instruct, single-needle NIAH on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, DBpedia/GloVe recall. No perplexity, no RULER/multi-needle, no Qwen, no ablation of TurboQuant_prod vs TurboQuant_mse on an LLM. **The KV sections never say which variant (mse or prod) is used, and never mention keys vs. values, RoPE, group size, or the stored float norms.**
- **No Conclusion, Limitations or Future-Work section exists.** The body ends with Section 4.4 / Figure 5 and goes straight to References.

## 1. Problem definition (Section 1.1, Eq. (1)–(2))

> "Formally, our goal is to design a quantization map, denoted as Q : R^d → {0,1}^B, that transforms d-dimensional vectors to a binary string of B bits. If we set B = b · d for some b ≥ 0, this quantizer will have a bit-width of b, representing the average number of bits used to encode each real-valued coordinate of R^d."

> "We make no assumptions about the input vector dataset, considering the worst-case scenario. We let the quantizer Q(·) to be randomized, leading to stochastic outputs. ... we aim to design quantizers that for any desired bit-width b minimize the following expected distortion measures for any (worst-case) vectors x, y ∈ R^d:
> (MSE)  D_mse := E_Q[ ‖x − Q⁻¹(Q(x))‖₂² ]   (1)
> (inner-prod error)  D_prod := E_Q[ |⟨y, x⟩ − ⟨y, Q⁻¹(Q(x))⟩|² ]   (2)
> The expectations above are takes with respect to the randomness of the quantizer Q(·)."

> "(unbiased inner-prod) E_Q[⟨y, Q⁻¹(Q(x))⟩] = ⟨y, x⟩."

Unit-norm convention (Section 1.3): "Note that the unit norm assumption, ‖x‖₂ = 1, is standard and not restrictive. For datasets that do not satisfy this assumption we can compute and store the L2 norms in floating-point precision and rescale the dequantized points using these stored norms." → all distortions below are **relative** (per unit ‖x‖²), and the stated bit-widths exclude the stored float norm(s).

## 2. Q1 — Lower bound: Theorem 3 (Section 3.3) and Lemmas 2–3 (Section 2.1)

**Theorem 3 (lower bound on best achievable compression distortion), verbatim:**
> "For any randomized quantization algorithm Q : S^{d−1} → {0,1}^{b·d} with bit-width b and any reconstruction map Q⁻¹ : {0,1}^{b·d} → R^d, there exist a hard input instance x ∈ S^{d−1} such that:
> D_mse(Q) := E[ ‖x − Q⁻¹(Q(x))‖₂² ] ≥ 1/4^b.
> Furthermore, there exists a y ∈ S^{d−1} such that:
> D_prod(Q) = E[ |⟨y, x⟩ − ⟨y, Q⁻¹(Q(x))⟩|² ] ≥ (1/d) · (1/4^b)."

Scope: **all** randomized quantizers (any map Q, any decoder Q⁻¹), not only data-oblivious ones. Distortion = squared ℓ₂ error on unit vectors (so relative MSE) for a *worst-case* input, expectation over the quantizer's randomness. Dependence: MSE bound 4^{−b} = 2^{−2b}, **independent of d**; inner-product bound (1/d)·4^{−b} for some unit y (overview restates it as (‖y‖₂²/d)·4^{−b}).

Proof (Section 3.3), verbatim core:
> "By Yao's minimax principle the expected MSE of the optimal randomized compression algorithm for worst-case inputs (D_mse) is equal to the expected MSE of the optimal deterministic compression algorithm when applied to inputs drawn from a maximally difficult randomized distribution. By definition, the MSE of the latter scenario is lower-bounded by the best achievable MSE for inputs uniformly distributed on the unit hypersphere. The best achievable MSE for a compression algorithm with bit-width b, operating on uniformly distributed inputs from the sphere S^{d−1}, is lower bounded in Lemma 3. Therefore, by invoking Lemma 3 we conclude that D_mse ≥ 1/4^b."
Inner-product part: D_mse = Σ_j E|⟨e_j, x⟩ − ⟨e_j, Q⁻¹(Q(x))⟩|² ≥ 4^{−b}; "By pigeonhole principle there exist an index j ∈ [d] such that E[...] ≥ (1/d)·(1/4^b)". So the hard y is a standard basis vector.

**Lemma 2 (SLB), verbatim:** "Let x ∈ R^d be a random vector with an arbitrary probability distribution p_X and finite differential entropy h(x). Define the MSE distortion-rate function D(B) for total bit complexity B ≥ 0 as: D(p_X, B) := inf{ E‖x − y‖₂² : I(x; y) ≤ B } ... Then, for any bit complexity B ≥ 0, the following Shannon Lower Bound holds: D(p_X, B) ≥ (d/2πe) · 2^{(2/d)(h(x) − B)}." (cited to Cover [14], "backward Gaussian test channel").

**Lemma 3 (SLB for random point on hypersphere), verbatim:** "Let x ∈ S^{d−1} be a random variable uniformly distributed over the unit hypersphere ... Then, for any bit complexity B ≥ 0, the following distortion lower bound holds: D(B) ≥ 2^{−2B/d}."
Proof: "h(x) = log₂ A_d ... D(B) ≥ (d/2πe)·A_d^{2/d}·2^{−2B/d}. Using Stirling's approximation formula for Gamma function we have A_d = 2π^{d/2}/Γ(d/2) ≥ √(2d/π)·(2πe/d)^{d/2}·(1 − O(1/d))."

Cold notes on Theorem 3:
- The Stirling step hides a (1 − O(1/d)) factor. Numerically (my computation, Section 6) the exact SLB constant (d/2πe)·A_d^{2/d} is 0.736 at d=2, 1.04 at d=4, 1.029 at d=128, 1.004 at d=1536 — so the stated bound D ≥ 4^{−b} is valid (slightly loose) for d ≥ 4 and false for d = 2, 3. For head-dim 128 the true SLB floor is 1.029·4^{−b}.
- The SLB is itself a lower bound on the Shannon distortion-rate function, which is achievable only as block length → ∞ (here the "block" is the d-vector), so Theorem 3 is an information-theoretic floor, not necessarily attainable at fixed d.
- Because it is a *worst-case / uniform-on-sphere* bound, it says nothing about structured data: a data-dependent (offline, calibrated) quantizer can beat 4^{−b} on anisotropic real KV caches whose differential entropy is lower than the sphere's. "Near-optimal" in the title means near-optimal for the worst-case/uniform source only. The theorem does not justify "data-oblivious is as good as data-dependent" on real data.
- The paper notes an alternative: "a comparable lower bound for the worst-case distortion in vector quantization can be derived using 'sphere packing' arguments (indeed, with larger constants as this is a harder problem) [26]."

## 3. Q2 — Achievability constant ≈ 2.7: Theorem 1 (Section 3.1) and how it is derived

**Algorithm 1 (TurboQuant_mse):** generate random rotation Π ∈ R^{d×d} ("by applying QR decomposition on a random matrix with i.i.d Normal entries" — not Hadamard in the paper); "Construct codebook by finding centroids c₁,…,c_{2^b} ∈ [−1,1] that minimize MSE cost in Eq. (4)"; Quant: y ← Πx, idx_j ← argmin_k |y_j − c_k|; DeQuant: ỹ_j ← c_{idx_j}, x̃ ← Πᵀỹ.

**Lemma 1 (coordinate distribution of random point on hypersphere), verbatim:** "if x ∈ S^{d−1} is a random variable uniformly distributed over the unit hypersphere, then for any j ∈ [d] the coordinate x_j follows the following (scaled/shifted) Beta distribution: x_j ∼ f_X(x) := Γ(d/2) / (√π · Γ((d−1)/2)) · (1 − x²)^{(d−3)/2}. In high dimensions this beta distribtion converges to the normal distribution f_X(·) → N(0, 1/d)."

Design statement (Section 3.1): "Furthermore, in high dimensions, distinct coordinates of Π·x become nearly independent [55], allowing us to apply optimal scalar quantizers to each coordinate independently. Therefore, by Lemma 1, our task reduces to designing a scalar quantizer for random variables with the distribution f_X ... The optimal scalar quantization problem, given a known probability distribution, can be framed as a continuous k-means problem in dimension one ... The optimal solution adheres to a Voronoi tessellation [42]".

Eq. (4): C(f_X, b) := min_{−1≤c₁≤…≤c_{2^b}≤1} Σ_{i=1}^{2^b} ∫_{(c_{i−1}+c_i)/2}^{(c_i+c_{i+1})/2} |x − c_i|² f_X(x) dx. "We solve Eq. (4) for a range of practically relevant bit-widths b once, and store the results". Example centroids (Gaussian limit): b=1: ±√(2/π)/√d; b=2: {±0.453/√d, ±1.51/√d}. (These are the standard Max-1960 Gaussian Lloyd–Max levels ±0.4528σ, ±1.510σ with σ = 1/√d.) Codebook depends only on (d, b): it is Lloyd–Max on the Beta/Gaussian marginal, i.e. **per-coordinate Lloyd–Max on the Beta marginal — confirmed.**

**Theorem 1 (performance guarantee: TurboQuant_mse), verbatim:** "For any bit-width b ≥ 1 and any vector x ∈ S^{d−1}, the procedure Quant_mse(x) in Algorithm 1 outputs an index vector idx ∈ [2^b]^d. When this index vector is passed to the primitive DeQuant_mse(idx), it produces a reconstructed vector x̃ ∈ R^d that satisfies the following distortion bounds:
> • MSE defined as D_mse := E_x̃[‖x − x̃‖₂²] is bounded by D_mse ≤ (√3π/2) · (1/4^b) for any b ≥ 0.
> • For small bit-widths, specifically b = 1, 2, 3, 4 the MSE exhibits finer-grained distortion values: D_mse ≈ 0.36, 0.117, 0.03, 0.009, respectively."

Proof structure (verbatim key lines): "We start the proof by showing that D_mse = d · C(f_X, b)" — via ‖x − x̃‖ = ‖Πx − ỹ‖, linearity of expectation over the d coordinates, and "all y_j's have identical distribution of y_j ∼ f_X(·) as shown in Lemma 1" (no independence needed). Then: "For moderate values of d, f_X → N(0, 1/d). By numerically solving the optimization problem in Eq. (4) for values b = 1, 2, 3, 4 we get that C(f_X, b) ≈ 0.36/d, 0.117/d, 0.03/d, 0.009/d, respectively. For larger bit-widths b > 4, we can apply the Panter–Dite [44] high-resolution formula for the distortion of a fixed-rate scalar quantizer, yielding the following bound: C(f_X, b) ≤ (1/12)·(∫ f_X(x)^{1/3} dx)³ · (1/4^b) = (√3π/(2d)) · (1/4^b)."

So the constant: **√3π/2 ≈ 2.7207 is the Panter–Dite high-rate constant of an optimal fixed-rate scalar (Lloyd–Max) quantizer on a Gaussian source**, (1/12)(∫f^{1/3})³ = (√3π/2)σ² for N(0,σ²); = 4.35 dB above the SLB (= 1.53 dB space-filling loss + 2.81 dB fixed-rate shaping loss of a Gaussian). It is asymptotic in b and in d. The paper's own constants give smaller gaps at low rate: D_mse/4^{−b} = 1.44 (b=1; paper: "approximately 1.45"), 1.87 (b=2), 1.92 (b=3), 2.30 (b=4) → 2.72 as b → ∞.

Overview statement (Section 1.3): "TurboQuant's MSE distortion is provably within a factor of at most √3π/2 ≈ 2.7 of the information-theoretical lower bound. Notably, for smaller bit-widths, this factor significantly decreases. For instance, at a bit-width of b = 1 TurboQuant achieves a distortion that is only a factor of approximately 1.45 away from the optimal".

**The ≈2.7 is for MSE only.** For the inner-product quantizer the paper's own bounds give a gap of √3π² ≈ 17.1 asymptotically (Theorem 2 constant / Theorem 3 constant), and 6.3, 9.0, 11.5, 12.0 at b = 1..4 from the paper's stated numbers. The abstract's "differing only by a small constant (≈ 2.7) factor" is accurate only for D_mse.

Entropy coding remark (Section 3.1): "The most significant reduction occurs for b = 4, where the entropy of {p_i} is approximately 3.8. Detailed calculations for optimal prefix codes reveal that the average bit-width can be reduced by 5%. However, given the limited gain, we have chosen not to incorporate this technique".

## 4. Q3 — QJL residual stage: Definition 1, Lemma 4 (Section 2.2), Algorithm 2, Theorem 2 (Section 3.2)

**Definition 1 (QJL), verbatim:** "For any positive integer d the QJL map Q_qjl : R^d → {−1,+1}^d is defined as: Q_qjl(x) := sign(S · x) for any x ∈ R^d, where S ∈ R^{d×d} is a random matrix with i.i.d. entries sampled from the normal distribution N(0,1) and the sign function is applied entry-wise ... The inverse/dequantization map Q_qjl⁻¹ : {−1,+1}^d → R^d is defined as: Q_qjl⁻¹(z) := (√(π/2) / d) · Sᵀ · z."
→ In TurboQuant the projection is **square (d×d)**: the sketch dimension m equals d; there is no m = 256 knob as in the QJL paper.

**Lemma 4 (performance guarantee: QJL), verbatim:** "For any vector x ∈ S^{d−1} and any y ∈ R^d we have the following:
> • Unbiased: E[⟨y, Q_qjl⁻¹(Q_qjl(x))⟩] = ⟨y, x⟩.
> • Variance Bound: Var(⟨y, Q_qjl⁻¹(Q_qjl(x))⟩) ≤ (π/2d) · ‖y‖₂²."
Proof: "The unbiasedness immediately follows from Lemma 3.2 of [62]"; estimator = (1/d) Σ_i √(π/2)·(s_iᵀy)·sign(s_iᵀx), i.i.d. terms z_i with "Var(z_i) = π/2 · Var(s_iᵀy · sign(s_iᵀx)) ≤ π/2 · E[(s_iᵀy)²] = π/2 · ‖y‖₂²  (3)", hence Var(average) ≤ (π/2d)‖y‖₂². (Note: the bound drops the −⟨y,x⟩² term, so it is an upper bound; the exact variance is [(π/2)‖y‖² − ⟨y,x⟩²]/d by the same computation.)

**Algorithm 2 (TurboQuant_prod):** "Instantiate a TurboQuant_mse with bit-width b − 1"; "Generate a random projection matrix S ∈ R^{d×d} with i.i.d. entries S_{i,j} ∼ N(0,1)"; Quant: idx ← Quant_mse(x); r ← x − DeQuant_mse(idx) {residual}; qjl ← sign(S·r); output (idx, qjl, ‖r‖₂). DeQuant(idx, qjl, γ): x̃_mse ← DeQuant_mse(idx); x̃_qjl ← (√(π/2)/d)·γ·Sᵀ·qjl; output x̃_mse + x̃_qjl.
Estimator (Section 3.2): "⟨y, Q_mse⁻¹(Q_mse(x))⟩ + ‖r‖₂ · ⟨y, Q_qjl⁻¹(Q_qjl(r))⟩". Output alphabet: "Q_prod : S^{d−1} → [2^{b−1}]^d × {−1,1}^d × R" — i.e. (b−1)·d bits + d sign bits + **one float (‖r‖₂) per vector**, plus the float ‖x‖₂ for non-unit inputs; neither float is counted in b.

Bias motivation (Section 3.2): for b = 1, Q_mse(x) = sign(Πx) with codebook ±√(2/(πd)), so "E[⟨y, Q_mse⁻¹(Q_mse(x))⟩] = (2/π)·⟨y, x⟩, which has a multiplicative bias of 2/π. This bias diminishes with increasing bit-widths b".

**Theorem 2 (performance guarantee: TurboQuant_prod), verbatim:** "For any bit-width b ≥ 1 and any vector x ∈ S^{d−1}, the procedure Quant_prod(x) in Algorithm 2 outputs an index vector idx ∈ [2^{b−1}]^d along with a sign vector qjl ∈ {−1,1}^d and a positive number γ ≥ 0. When these ... are passed to ... DeQuant_prod(idx, qjl, γ), it produces a reconstructed vector x̃ ∈ R^d that for any vector y ∈ R^d satisfies:
> • Expected inner-product E_x̃[⟨y, x̃⟩] = ⟨y, x⟩
> • Inner-product distortion defined as D_prod := E_x̃[|⟨y, x⟩ − ⟨y, x̃⟩|²] is bounded by D_prod ≤ (√3π² · ‖y‖₂² / d) · (1/4^b) for any b ≥ 0.
> • For small bit-widths, specifically b = 1, 2, 3, 4, D_prod exhibits finer-grained distortion values: D_prod ≈ 1.57/d, 0.56/d, 0.18/d, 0.047/d, respectively."
(These are for ‖y‖ = 1 implicitly.)

Proof core (verbatim): conditional on x̃_mse, "E[|⟨y,x⟩ − ⟨y,x̃⟩|² | x̃_mse] = Var(⟨y, x̃_qjl⟩ | x̃_mse) ≤ (π/2d)·‖r‖₂²‖y‖₂²"; then "D_prod ≤ (π/2d)·‖y‖₂²·E[‖x − x̃_mse‖₂²] = (π/2d)·‖y‖₂²·D_mse. The theorem follows by invoking the MSE bounds from Theorem 1 with bit-width b − 1."

So the **exact variance structure of the residual stage** is: conditional variance = (π/2d)·‖y‖²·‖r‖² where r is the realised (per-vector) Lloyd–Max residual with E‖r‖² = D_mse(b−1) ∈ {1, 0.36, 0.117, 0.03} for b−1 = 0..3. Consequence (my arithmetic from the paper's constants): spending the last bit on QJL instead of Lloyd–Max costs a factor D_prod·d / D_mse(b) = 4.4, 4.8, 6.0, 5.2 at b = 1..4 and 2π ≈ 6.3 asymptotically in inner-product variance, in exchange for unbiasedness. The paper's own Section 4.1 concedes the practical side: "for inner product estimation, the TurboQuant_prod approach performs better at lower bit ratios. However, as the bit count increases, TurboQuant_mse reduces bias and ultimately achieves superior performance in inner product estimation." (Figure 3(a).)

## 5. Q4 — Empirical bits/channel claims (Section 4.2–4.3, Table 1, Figure 4)

Setup: "All experiments are performed using a single NVIDIA A100 GPU." KV sections do not state whether TurboQuant_mse or TurboQuant_prod is used.

Non-integer bit-widths (Section 4.3, verbatim): "We evaluate our method using 2.5-bit and 3.5-bit quantization during text generation. These non-integer bit precisions result from our strategy of splitting channels into outlier and non-outlier sets, and applying two independent instances of TurboQuant to each, allocating higher bit precision to outliers. This outlier treatment strategy is consistent with prior work [63, 51]. For example, in our 2.5-bit setup, 32 outlier channels are quantized at 3 bits, while the remaining 96 channels use 2 bits, leading to an effective bit precision of (32 × 3 + 96 × 2)/128 = 2.5. For 3.5-bit quantization, a different ratio of outliers and regular channels leads to a higher effective bit precision." (The 3.5-bit split is not specified. Outlier-channel selection is data-dependent, which sits uneasily with "data-oblivious".) "Unlike existing approaches such as KIVI and PolarQuant, which leave generated tokens unquantized, our method applies quantization even during the streaming generation process." "Remarkably, we achieve this while compressing quantized vectors by at least a factor of 4.5×."

**Table 1** (caption: "LongBench-V1 [10] results of various KV cache compression methods on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct"; text says the LongBench-E subset is used). Columns: KV Size (bits) | SingleQA | MultiQA | Summarization | Few shot | Synthetic | Code | Average.

So "absolute quality neutrality with 3.5 bits per channel" = **average 50.06 vs 50.06 on one model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) on LongBench-E**, with per-category deltas of −0.28, +0.15, −0.55, +0.25, +0.41, −0.11. Note KIVI at 5 bits (50.16) beats the full cache and TurboQuant-3.5; the text's "achieving significantly higher average scores" is not supported by the table beyond the 3-bit KIVI row.

## 7. Paper's own limitations / future work
