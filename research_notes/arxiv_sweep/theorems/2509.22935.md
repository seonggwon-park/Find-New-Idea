## 1. Nothing is proved: two fitted empirical laws plus an LR-schedule heuristic

### Problem definition (§3)
D*_qat(N, D_total, B) = argmin over D_qat + D_fp = D_total of L(N, D_fp, D_qat, B), and f* = D*_qat / D_total.

### (a) Direct optimal-fraction fit (§3.1; verified in the HTML)

f̂(D_total, N, B) = exp( log S_total − a / log S_total ) / S_total = **exp(−a / ln S_total)**, where S_total = D_total / (N · B/8) and **a = 6.7297**.

- S_total is "tokens-per-parameter-byte": B is the QAT bit width, so N·B/8 is bytes.
- This is a single-parameter fit, done with Huber loss. "The approach achieves 0.091 MAE in fraction prediction across all model sizes and experiments." It was also checked by holding out the largest model.
- Evaluated values (my computation):

  | S_total | 10 | 100 | 1,000 | 10,000 | 100,000 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | f̂ | 0.054 | 0.232 | 0.377 | 0.482 | 0.557 |

  - Example: an 8B model trained on 15T tokens has S = 3,750 for 4-bit QAT, giving f̂ ≈ 0.44. At 2-bit, S = 7,500 and f̂ ≈ 0.47.
- Paper's observations: "the optimal QAT fraction grows faster with D_total for lower bit widths, the optimal QAT fraction decreases with model size N increase and fixed D_total".
- Takeaway 1: "Optimal QAT compute allocation fraction is not stationary but grows with total training tokens-per-parameter-byte (S_total) and can be predicted from it."
- Stated limits of this fit: "subject to the granularity of the selected experiments—the set of QAT fractions being tested". It also ignores the non-optimal data points.

### (b) Loss scaling law (Eq. 3.1)

L(N, D_qat, D_fp, B) = α + β / D_total^γ + ζ / N^η + δ, with

δ = θ·2^{−κB} [irreducible QAT error] + φ·2^{−χB} / (N^ψ · S_qat^ω) [pure QAT penalty] + λ·2^{−μB} / (N^ν · S_fp^ξ · S_qat^ρ) [FP/QAT interaction],

where S_qat = D_qat / (N·B/8) and S_fp = D_fp / (N·B/8).

Fitted values (App. D, Fig. 8, verified in the HTML):

L = 1.598 + 2477.0/D_total^0.4089 + 57.64/N^0.2148 + 0.4297·2^{−1.41B} + 1091.0·2^{−1.212B} / (N^0.4004·S_qat^0.076) + 138.8·2^{−0.0833B} / (N^0.2135·S_fp^0.4819·S_qat^0.1903).

- Fit data: 757 QAT runs, fitted with Huber loss and gradient descent. "The results are highly dependent on initialization; therefore, we select the best fit out of many random initializations."
- Full precision is included as B = 16, which also acts as a regularizer (App. F). Samples are reweighted by inverse bit-width frequency.
- Table 1 fit quality:

  | B | MAE (loss) | R² (loss) | MAE of inferred f* |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 0.026 | 0.982 | 0.081 |
  | 2 | 0.023 | 0.981 | 0.102 |
  | 4 | 0.021 | 0.983 | 0.074 |
  | 6 | 0.018 | 0.991 | 0.09 |

- Why the law's form differs from Chen et al. (2025b; 2505.14302): in that law "the QAT penalty overtakes the Chinchilla loss term ... the limit of the whole expression to approach infinity as D_total → ∞". The new form keeps the loss decreasing as tokens grow.
- Critique: the FP/QAT interaction term has an S_fp exponent of 0.4819. More FP tokens therefore shrink the penalty. Whether f* rises then comes down to the relative exponents, which are fitted values, not derived ones. Also, the 2^{−0.0833B} factor is almost flat in B.
- The paper concedes the law fails at low token counts (App. E): "the optimal QAT fraction appears to be lower than the predicted one".

### Data scope
- Model sizes: 86.0M–759.0M are used for fitting. A 2.2B model is used only as a held-out check (App. K, Table 9; "2.9 times larger than the largest model in the loss scaling law fit data").
- Total tokens: 2.3B–1.4T. Bit widths: 1, 2, 4 and 6.
- Training: DCLM data, Llama-2 architecture, sequence length 1024. FP training uses WSD with a 20% 1−sqrt cooldown.
- QAT: initialized from the post-cooldown FP checkpoint, with a cosine schedule (5% warmup, decay to 0).
- **Weight-only quantization**: ParetoQ quantizers (Elastic Binarization for 1-bit, SEQ for 2-bit, LSQ for ≥3-bit), per-output-feature scales, embeddings at ≥4 bits (App. A.2–A.4). No activation or KV quantization appears anywhere.

## 2. Further law-derived claims (inferences from the fitted law, not direct measurements)

- Takeaway 3 (§4.1, Fig. 4): compared with a fixed 10% QAT fraction, "for 1-bit QAT, the same loss can be achieved with just around 50% of compute if the optimal QAT fraction is used". The effect "becomes relatively small for 6-bit".
  - This uses a 1B model, which is an extrapolation beyond the 759M fit range.
- Takeaway 4: the law predicts the range of tokens over which QAT matches FP, and "larger-parameter models can tolerate lower-bit QAT". Figure 5 extrapolates to 16B parameters and 100T tokens.
- Takeaway 5 (Fig. 6): "for a fixed memory budget, optimal QAT precision decreases with training FLOP growth". This is checked experimentally in App. I.
- QAT overhead: compute is treated as equal to tokens. The QAT overhead factor r can be folded in via D_qat = D'_qat / r. The overhead is "negligible with sufficiently large batch size and sequence length" (App. L).

## 3. QAT and LR cooldown fusion (§5): exact claim and the part that does not hold

- Motivation (l.801–803): "Motivated by the idea that learning rate cooldown performs low-magnitude adjustments to weights, we speculate that a substantial part of updates during learning rate cooldown gets effectively lost". The word used is *speculate*; the paper does not measure this.
- Scheme: QAT starts from the constant-LR stage, and the LR cooldown runs during QAT. Fig. 7 shows a 40% QAT fraction with a 20% cooldown. This replaces the usual sequence of FP cooldown followed by QAT re-warmup.
- Main-text Table 2 covers **4-bit only**. Perplexity goes from unfused to fused as follows; "wasted tokens" is the token saving read off the scaling law (App. O).

  | Model | D_total | Unfused PPL | Fused PPL | Change | Wasted tokens |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 74M | 1.4T | 16.26 | 16.25 | −0.06% | 2.2% |
  | 163M | 901.3B | 13.51 | 13.49 | −0.15% | 9.2% |
  | 425M | 10.5B | 16.3 | 16.02 | −1.72% | 9.6% |
  | 425M | 31.8B | 13.9 | 13.76 | −1.01% | 10.4% |
  | 425M | 96.0B | 12.62 | 12.54 | −0.63% | 13.6% |
  | 816M | 281.9B | 11.07 | 11.02 | −0.45% | 13.2% |

- **Appendix Table 10 (HTML) shows the fusion is not uniformly beneficial.**
  - 6-bit: improves in all 9 settings, with wasted tokens 9.1–38.8%.
  - **1-bit: worse in 5 of 7 settings** (wasted tokens −19.5%, −5.3%, −8.1%, −26.8%, −6.9%; better only at 425M/11.1B with +4.4% and 816M/22.2B with +7.3%).
  - **2-bit: worse in 4 of 11**: 74M/70.4B (−7.1%), 74M/323.6B (−12.4%), 163M/65.0B (−2.3%) and 425M/305.8B (−5.9%). The other seven settings improve by 2.0–13.3%; the smallest of these, 816M/297.5B, has a −0.00% PPL change but is listed at +2.0% wasted tokens.
  - The paper's explanation: "the large optimal QAT fraction for lower bits, which minimizes the impact".
  - So the abstract's "achieving significant compute savings" holds for 4- and 6-bit weight-only QAT. It does not hold for 1–2 bit.
- No seeds or error bars are reported. Perplexity differences of 0.06–0.15% at 74M and 163M are within plausible run-to-run noise.

## 4. Limitations and future work (verbatim)

- §6 scope: "our work still focuses on a specific LLM architecture, and exact results may differ for different model types. However, we expect the overall observed phenomena to be consistent across different architectures and datasets."
- Future work: "First, the relationship between the optimal QAT fraction and pretraining precision remains unknown. This direction is especially interesting with the emergence of 8-bit floating-point training ... and even 4-bit training ... Second, ... how does the optimal QAT fraction change when the full-precision training stage incorporates additional stages such as SFT, RL, or multimodal training?"
- App. R.1 speculates: "one may expect the optimal QAT fraction to be smaller when a model is pretrained in lower floating-point precisions (fp4, fp8) than in high precision ... Still, we expect the optimal QAT fraction to grow with increasing total compute."

   - It gives an empirical timing law, f* growing with S_total, that any switch criterion should be compared against.
