## 0. What kind of paper this is

Grep for `Theorem|Lemma|Proposition|Corollary|Proof` over the full text returns **zero hits**. SpinQuant contains no theorem, no bound, no convergence or generalization statement. Its content is (i) a rotation parameterization that keeps the FP network numerically invariant, (ii) an empirical observation that random rotations give high-variance quantized accuracy, (iii) an optimization recipe (Cayley SGD on the Stiefel manifold against the quantized network's task loss), and (iv) experiments. The only "analysis" is Appendix B: a one-layer gradient expression (Eq. 5) and SQNR measurements (Table 19, Fig. 7).

## 1. The "up to 13 points" spread — exact statements (Q1)

Abstract (line 18–20):
> "we find that some random rotations lead to much better quantization than others, with an up to 13 points difference in downstream zero-shot reasoning performance."

Introduction (line 169–171):
> "In addition to using random rotation, which statistically works well, we find that the performance of quantized network could vary a lot with different rotation matrices. For example, the downstream averaged accuracy on zero-shot reasoning tasks may change up to 13 points with different rotations."

Section 2.2 "Random rotations produce large variance" (line 295–304) — the actual evidence:
> "Interestingly, while statistically random rotation leads to better quantization, not all random rotations give the same quantization outcome. To show this, we tested the zero-shot average accuracy of the rotated version of LLaMA-2 7B, quantized to 4-bit weight and 4-bit activation, under 100 randomized trials. As shown in Figure 4, the performance variance is substantial, with the best random rotation matrix outperforming the worst by 13 points. Random Hadamard matrices [footnote 1] outperform random rotation matrices, in consistent with the findings in (Tseng et al., 2024) that Hadamard matrices yield tighter bounds on weight maximal value. However, even random Hadamard rotation matrices exhibit a non-negligible variance in final performance, as large as 6 points."

Figure 4 caption (line 286–291):
> "The performance distributions of W4A4 quantized LLaMA-2 7B under different random rotations, using network-level parameterization (Sec. 3.1). We compare the distributions using random floating-point rotations, random Hadamard matrices, and optimized rotation matrices with Cayley optimization (Sec. 3.2). Despite that Hadamard matrices mostly perform better than random rotations, both random groups demonstrate large variance. In contrast, by optimizing the rotation matrix with Cayley optimization (i.e., SpinQuant), the performance is improved significantly and the variance becomes much smaller."
(Figure 4 is a box/violin-style plot; y-axis "Zero-shot Reasoning Accuracy (Avg.)" with ticks 40–60; three groups "Random", "Hadamard", "Cayley optimized". The individual values are not tabulated in the paper.)

Footnote 1 (line 329–331) — what "random Hadamard" means here:
> "A Hadamard matrix H is a special type of rotation matrix, where the entries of the matrix are solely ±1/√n. Given a Hadamard matrix H, we can generate 2^n different random Hadamard matrices by multiplying with S, a diagonal matrix with elements s_i randomly chosen from {−1, 1}."

Section 3.2 (line 537–541) — the comparison point for the learned rotation:
> "By employing Cayley optimization to update the rotation for 100 iterations on an 800-sample WikiText2 calibration dataset, we obtain a rotation matrix that outperforms the best random matrix and random Hadamard matrix in 100 random seeds, shown in Figure 4. The Cayley-optimized rotation exhibits minimal variance when initiated from different random seeds."

### Cold reading of Q1
- Model/setting: **LLaMA-2 7B, W4A4 (KV not quantized, i.e. W4A4KV16 by Table 4's layout), 100 random trials, metric = average accuracy on eight zero-shot commonsense tasks (BoolQ, PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-e, ARC-c, OBQA), rotations R1 (residual, D_token×D_token) and R2 (head-wise) of the Sec. 3.1 parameterization.** The weight quantizer for Figure 4 is not named in Sec. 2.2; Table 4, which is the tabulated version of the same experiment, uses RTN.
- The **13-point** figure is the best-minus-worst range over 100 "random floating-point rotations" = "random orthogonal floating-point rotation matrices". The paper **never uses the word "Haar"** and never states how the random orthogonal matrices were sampled (QR of Gaussian vs. other). It is a max-min range, not a std; the corresponding std is ±2.8 points (Table 4, 4-4-16).
- For **random Hadamard** (sign-randomized, 2^n family) the spread is **6 points** (std ±1.0). Hadamard's mean is ~10 points above the random-FP mean (59.0 vs 49.4), i.e., most of the random-FP variance is in a low tail that Hadamard does not have.
- The paper itself attributes the Hadamard advantage to QuIP#'s tighter max-entry bound ("Hadamard matrices yield tighter bounds on weight maximal value"), so it does NOT present the 13-point spread as evidence against incoherence theory; it presents it as motivation to learn R.
- The "up to 16.2 points" improvement in the contributions list is a different number: Table 2, Mistral-7B W4A4KV4, random Hadamard R{1,2,3,4} 52.4 → SpinQuant_had 68.6 (↑16.2); 4-4-16: 52.7 → 68.4 (↑15.7). Other models in Table 2 gain only 0.9–3.3 points.

## 2. Objective on the Stiefel manifold and theory given (Q2)

Section 3.2 "Cayley-optimized rotation", Eq. (2) (line 475–492):
> "Given that R3 and R4 are online rotation operations, meaning they cannot be absorbed into the weight matrix, we retain them as Hadamard matrices. ... We then define the optimization objective as identifying the optimal rotation matrix R1 and R2 that minimizes the final loss of the quantized network:
>   arg min_{R∈M} L_Q(R1, R2 | W, X)        (2)
> Here, M represents the Stiefel manifold i.e., the set of all orthonormal matrices. L_Q(·) denotes the task loss, such as cross-entropy, on the calibration set. It is a function of {R1, R2}, given the fixed pretrained weights W and the input tensor X and with the quantization function Q in the network."

Update rule, Eq. (3)–(4) (line 493–531):
> "we employ the Cayley SGD method (Li et al., 2020) ... in each iteration, the update of the rotation R is parameterized as the following:
>   R' = ΔR(Y) R := (I − (α/2) Y)^{-1} (I + (α/2) Y) R        (3)
> where ΔR(Y) := (I − (α/2)Y)^{-1}(I + (α/2)Y) is the Cayley Transform of a skew-symmetric matrix Y (i.e., Y^T = −Y). Y is computed from a projection Ĝ of the gradient G := ∇_R L_Q of the loss function:
>   Y = Ĝ − Ĝ^T,   Ĝ := G R^T − (1/2) R R^T G R^T        (4)
> It can be shown that ΔR(Y) is always orthonormal and thus R' is guaranteed to be orthonormal (R'^T R' = I) if R is orthonormal. While Eqn. 3 requires a matrix inverse, the new rotation matrix R' can be computed via an efficient fixed point iteration (Li et al., 2020). Overall, the approach maintains the property of orthonormality with only ∼2 times the computation time per iteration compared to a naive SGD algorithm."

Line 533–536: "We apply the Cayley SGD method to solve Eqn. 2 for {R1, R2}, while the underlying weight parameters in the network remain frozen. {R1, R2} count for only ∼0.26% of the weight size and is constrained to be orthonormal. Consequently, the underlying floating-point network remains unchanged, and the rotation only influences the quantization performance."

What is actually optimized in the main results — Section 4.1 (line 556–569) and 4.3.2 (line 1077–1087):
> "We employ Cayley SGD (Li et al., 2020) to optimize the rotation matrix, R1 and R2, both initialized as a random Hadamard matrix, while maintaining all network weights constant. R1 is the residual rotation, shaped as (D_token, D_token). R2 is head-wise rotation in each attention block, shaped as (D_head, D_head) and is separately learned in each layer. The learning rate starts at 1.5 and linearly decays to 0. We utilize 800 samples from WikiText-2 to optimize rotation for 100 iterations."
> "In the main results, we optimize the rotation with respect to the activation quantized network, where the weights remain 16-bit. After rotation is learned, we apply GPTQ on the rotated weights ... 128 samples from WikiText2 with a sequence length of 2048 as the calibration set for GPTQ."
> Table 3 (LLaMA-2 7B): optimizing R against the 4-4-KV network gives 61.0 ±1.0 / Wiki 6.7 ±0.07 (4-4-16) vs. optimizing against the 16-4-KV network (then GPTQ) 64.1 ±0.4 / 5.9 ±0.00.
So the headline objective is the cross-entropy of the **activation(+KV)-quantized, 16-bit-weight** network; weight quantization is handled afterwards by GPTQ, not inside L_Q. Sequence length used for rotation optimization is NOT stated (only GPTQ's 128×2048 is).

Optimization time (Sec. 4.1, Table 15): ~13/18/30 min for LLaMA-3 1B/3B/8B, ~25/30 min LLaMA-2 7B/13B, ~3.5 h LLaMA-2 70B, ~16 min Mistral-7B.

### Is any theory given? — No theorem. Two pieces of informal analysis:

(a) Appendix B.1 "Gradient analysis", Eq. (5) (line 3421–3430, 3482–3489) — a single quantized linear layer:
> "we inspect the gradient of the output of a single linear, W, and activations, X, which are both rotated and quantized:
>   ∂ Σ_ij [Q(W R^{-1}) Q(R X)]_ij / ∂R_mn = Σ_ij −(W R^{-1})_im (R^{-1} Q(RX))_nj + Q(W R^{-1})_im X_nj        (5)
> We see that equation (5): • is non-zero in general, which validates our approach of using backpropagation to learn R • reduces to 0 when quantization is not present, which validates the claim that it only makes sense to learn R for quantized models • demonstrates that two components move the gradient with respect to R away from 0: 1) differences in quantized and unquantized rotated weights; 2) differences in quantized and unquantized rotated activations"
(Note: this treats Q as if it were differentiated through; the paper never names a straight-through estimator or says how ∇_R passes through Q. The extraction of Eq. 5 is garbled; the structure above is reconstructed.)

(b) Appendix B.2 "Loss analysis" + Table 19 (line 3660–3668, 3497–3515): end-to-end SQNR (dB) on WikiText2 test, LLaMA-2 7B W4A4: **R = I: −2.9 dB; randomly initialized R: 0.9 dB; learned R: 6.8 dB** ("an SNR improvement of 3.8 dB when introducing a random R ... and then an additional 5.9 dB improvement after learning R"). Figure 7c: per-layer activation SNR "improves significantly for a few layers, but does not change much for most layers, and even gets worse for one of the layers. We hypothesize that: 1) certain layers have a disproportionate impact on model output or have a disproportionately low quantization SNR without rotation; 2) The process of optimizing R rotates the residual stream basis such as to prioritize improving the SNR of such layers, possibly at the cost of hurting less important layers."

(c) Section 2.1 motivation only: kurtosis of activations κ > 200 in many layers before rotation, ≈ 3 after random rotation (Figure 3a); "a random rotation matrix statistically blends large and small weights together into a well-behaved distribution with fewer outliers (Elhage et al., 2023)".

(d) Section 4.4 "Illustrative analysis": 2-D/3-D toy picture (45° rotation equalizes ranges; 3-D example [2, 0.5, 0.5] → [1, 1, 1.414]). Explicitly leaves the theory open: "This opens up intriguing research avenues, such as determining if, given an activation distribution with known outlier axes and magnitudes, a closed-form solution for the optimal rotation matrix that evenly distributes magnitude across different axes can be derived. Additionally, it raises the question of whether this theoretically calculated rotation yields the best quantization performance. We leave this question to future research."

Stated properties that ARE exact (but trivial/standard): orthonormality is preserved by the Cayley update (Li et al. 2020); the FP network output is invariant to R1, R2 (pre-norm LLaMA with RMSNorm scale folded into the following weights, footnote 2 citing Ashkboos et al. 2023a = SliceGPT); {R1,R2} ≈ 0.26 % of the weight count.

## 4. The paper's own limitations / future-work statements (verbatim or close)

There is no "Limitations" section. The only explicit forward-looking statements are:
1. Sec. 4.4 (line 1229–1233, 1276–1277): "This opens up intriguing research avenues, such as determining if, given an activation distribution with known outlier axes and magnitudes, a closed-form solution for the optimal rotation matrix that evenly distributes magnitude across different axes can be derived. Additionally, it raises the question of whether this theoretically calculated rotation yields the best quantization performance. We leave this question to future research."
2. Appendix C (line 3672–3681): "in several activation layers, the first token displays substantial values in multiple channels. After rotation, this outlier is distributed across all channels of the first token. Although per-token activation quantization can readily manage this distribution, investigating the source of these outliers and reducing them prior to applying SpinQuant might further enhance quantization accuracy, which could be a potential future research direction."
3. Appendix B.2 (line 3508–3515): the per-layer SNR finding ("gets worse for one of the layers") is left as a hypothesis, not explained.
4. Sec. 4.5: online Hadamard (SpinQuant_had) costs "a modest 8% increase in latency" on M1 Pro CPU; trade-off left to the user.
Implicit limitations the paper does not discuss: no statement about how gradients pass through Q; no held-out vs. calibration loss gap reported for the rotation; the 13-point spread is only for one model (LLaMA-2 7B) and one bit setting; random-rotation sampling procedure unspecified.

## 5. What the report must change

Current citation (reports/LLM 양자화 연구 아이디어 10선.md, line 39): "SpinQuant가 보고한 "Haar 회전 간 13점 편차"는 비간섭성 보장이 손실을 통제하지 못한다는 직접 증거다 ([SpinQuant 2405.16406] [V])."

1. **Drop "Haar".** The paper never says Haar. Say "100개의 무작위 직교(부동소수) 회전 간 최대-최소 13점 편차 (LLaMA-2 7B, W4A4, 8개 zero-shot 평균, Sec. 2.2 / Fig. 4; 표준편차 ±2.8, Table 4 RTN)". If the report wants the Hadamard family, the number is **6 points** (std ±1.0), and that is the family QuIP#/QuaRot actually use for incoherence.
2. **Do not call it "직접 증거" that incoherence bounds fail to control loss.** The paper (i) says random Hadamard beats random orthogonal "in consistent with ... Hadamard matrices yield tighter bounds on weight maximal value" (i.e., it reads the data as supporting the QuIP# bound), (ii) gives no incoherence measurement for any rotation, and (iii) proves nothing. At most it is evidence that *within* a family of rotations with (presumably) similar incoherence the end-task accuracy still varies by 6–13 points, which is consistent with, not a refutation of, a worst-case bound. Rephrase as "정황 증거" and attach the Hadamard-vs-random distinction.
3. **Note the setting is RTN-weight / W4A4 on one 7B model**, where random-rotation failures are dominated by a low tail (mean 49.4 vs Hadamard 59.0); after learning the std collapses to ±0.3–0.8.
4. **Lines 201 and 213 (overfitting hypothesis).** The report's "P ≈ 8×10⁶ (d=4096)" for R1 is right in order of magnitude (d(d−1)/2 ≈ 8.4M; the paper says {R1,R2} ≈ 0.26 % of weights), but "캘리브레이션 ≈ 1.6×10⁶ 토큰 (800×2048)" is an inference: the paper states 800 WikiText-2 samples and 100 iterations but **not** the sequence length used for rotation optimization (2048 is stated only for GPTQ). More importantly, the paper's own ablations cut against a large calibration-generalization gap for the rotation: Table 11 shows 128 vs 800 samples give identical Wiki PPL (6.2 ±0.03), and Table 13 shows C4 vs WikiText2 calibration gives consistent results. The report should cite these as counter-evidence that any experiment must beat, not omit them.
5. **If the report anywhere says SpinQuant minimizes a layer-wise MSE or an incoherence objective — it does not.** Eq. (2) is the end-to-end task loss (cross-entropy) of the activation-quantized network on the calibration set, weights frozen, R3/R4 fixed Hadamard, optimized with Cayley SGD (Li et al. 2020); GPTQ is applied afterwards. The only "theory" is the one-layer gradient expression Eq. (5) in Appendix B.1 and SQNR numbers (Table 19).
