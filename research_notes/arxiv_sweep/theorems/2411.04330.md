## 0. One-paragraph cold summary

This paper **proves nothing**. There is no theorem, lemma or proposition anywhere in the main text or appendices; the only "derivations" (Appendix E) are first-order-condition manipulations of an *assumed* parametric loss. Every result is a **parametric fit** to 465 small pretraining runs (N ∈ {30, 60, 110, 220}M non-embedding, D ∈ {1.5, 3, 6, 13, 26}B tokens, OLMo-style Transformer++ on Dolma) with **simulated integer quantization of the forward pass only**, plus one floating-point validation sweep at 220M–1.6B (abstract says "up to 1.7B"). The core modeling device is an "effective parameter count" N_eff = N·∏(1 − e^{−P_x/γ_x}) substituted into Chinchilla, and a PTQ-degradation term δ_PTQ ∝ C_T (D^{γ_D}/N^{γ_N}) e^{−P_post/γ_post}. Reported fit quality: R² = 0.97 (Fig. 2, PTQ vs data), R² = 0.9655 (Fig. 5, P_w marginal), R² = 0.90 on >1000 points for the unified δ_PTQ (Fig. 7). The "7-8 bits compute-optimal" statement is an *implication of the fitted integer-type law*, and the authors explicitly say the fitted constants "are unlikely to be useful" (App. K) and that the trends are "suggestive rather than prescriptive" (Sec. 6). The mechanism section (App. H) opens with "This section is speculative."

## 1. Exact functional forms (Q1)

### 1.1 Notation (Sec. 2, lines 172–179)
> "Throughout, D denotes dataset size in tokens and N denotes model size in parameters. P_w, P_a, P_kv refer to the bit precision, **in integer-type**, of the weights, activations, and key-value cache ("attention") during training, and P_post refers to the precision we post-train quantize (PTQ) weights to at the end for model inference. When P or P_train is used without reference to a part of the model, all three model parts are tied to the same precision. The inference-time loss degradation induced by post-train quantization will be denoted δ_PTQ(N, D, P_train, P_post), and it is defined as the change in loss from performing post-training quantization compared to the end of pretraining. We use "high precision" to mean 16-bit or above."

### 1.2 Eq. (1) — top-level unified form (Sec. 1, line ~146)
> L(N, D, P_train, P_post) = A·N_eff^{−α} + B·D^{−β} + E + δ_PTQ(N_eff, D, P_train, P_post)
> "where A, B, E, α, β are positive fitted constants, and δ_PTQ refers to the loss degradation induced by post-training quantization before inference."
(First term labeled "Training-time Effects", first three terms "Usual Chinchilla form", last term "Post-Training Effects".)

### 1.3 Eq. (2) / Finding 1 — PTQ degradation for BF16-trained models (Sec. 3.1, lines 342–373)
> δ_PTQ(N, D, P_post) = C_T · (D^{γ_D} / N^{γ_N}) · e^{−P_post/γ_post}      (2)
> "where C_T, γ_D, γ_N, γ_post are positive fitted constants. As we find the fitted values of γ_D and γ_N to be similar (see Appendix K for numerical values), we can think of this as an approximate power law in the token/parameter ratio D/N."

Finding 1 (verbatim box, lines 365–373): "Overtrained language models are more sensitive to post-training quantization. For models trained in BF16 or above, we can model this loss degradation as [Eq. 2] … This implies that when D/N is sufficiently large, or P_post sufficiently small, loss after quantization can increase as models are pretrained for longer, as in Figure 2."

Fit quality stated in Fig. 2 caption (line 339): "R² = 0.97 over all fitted points (bottom row)."

### 1.4 Eq. (3) — N_eff for weights only (QAT) (Sec. 4.1, lines 400–407)
> "we find the best fit for the tradeoff between weight precision and parameters is N_eff(N, P_w) = N(1 − e^{−P_w/γ_w}), where γ_w is a fitted constant measuring the sensitivity of model weights (alternative fits explored in Appendix K). We therefore modify Chinchilla scaling to account for N_eff by making the substitution N ↦ N_eff(N, P_w), giving the modified form:
> L(N, D) = A[N(1 − e^{−P_w/γ_w})]^{−α} + B·D^{−β} + E      (3)"

Empirical observation motivating it (line 398): "the gains from increasing the bit precision of the weights are large at lower precisions but saturate at higher precisions (typically around 6-7 bits per weight)." Fit done at fixed D = 13B tokens (line 393).

### 1.5 Eq. (4) / Finding 2 — three-way multiplicative N_eff (Sec. 4.2, lines 619–718)
> N_eff(P) = N(1 − e^{−P_w/γ_w})(1 − e^{−P_a/γ_a})(1 − e^{−P_kv/γ_kv})      (4)

Finding 2 (verbatim box, lines 711–719): "The effects of quantizing the weights, activations, and KV cache during training are well modeled as independent and multiplicative so that
L(N, D, P_w, P_a, P_kv) = A·N_eff^{−α} + B·D^{−β} + E
where N_eff(P_w, P_a, P_kv) = N(1 − e^{−P_w/γ_w})(1 − e^{−P_a/γ_a})(1 − e^{−P_kv/γ_kv})
for which we fit constants γ_w, γ_a, γ_kv that reflect the different sensitivities of weights, activations, and KV cache. If the three precisions are set to the same value P, as in pretraining, this simplifies to N_eff(P) ≈ N(1 − e^{−P/γ̄})³ where γ̄ is the average of the three parameters."

Justification for independence is purely predictive (lines 702–708): "comparing the predictive power of a model with marginally fitted constants and a model with jointly fitted constants … both methods for fitting constants have approximately the same predictive power. These results suggest that the independence assumption is reasonable." Fig. 5 left reports "P_w Marginal Sweep MSE: 0.0028, R²: 0.9655". App. M (line ~2430) concedes: "we acknowledge that alternative factorizations that do not decompose the model into weights, activations, and KV cache, may have an equally good fit."

### 1.6 Eq. (5) — cost model used for compute-optimality (Sec. 4.3, lines 723–734)
> "When training in a precision P, meaning P_w = P_a = P_kv = P, compute cost scales linearly in P … We generalize this to C = (6/16)·N·D·P … which reduces to the Chinchilla cost function for P = 16."
> min_{N,D,P} L(N, D, P) = A[N(1 − e^{−P/γ})³]^{−α} + B·D^{−β} + E   subject to   C = (6/16)·N·D·P      (5)
> "Since derivations are algebraically involved, we will work up to proportionality and verify proposed solutions numerically."

### 1.7 Eqs. (6)–(7) — compute-optimal allocation and precision (Sec. 4.3.1–4.3.2, lines 853–896)
Eq. (6): N*(P,C)/N_Ch(C) ∝ [1 − e^{−P/γ̄}]^{−3α/(α+β)} · P^{−β/(α+β)} and D*(P,C)/D_Ch(C) ∝ [1 − e^{−P/γ̄}]^{3α/(α+β)} · P^{−α/(α+β)}, "which suggests as precision of training decreases at fixed compute, we should increase parameters and decrease data."
Eq. (7): with u(P) = [1 − e^{−P/γ̄}]^{−3α}, "(3α/γ̄)·u(P)^{(3α+1)/(3α)}·e^{−P/γ̄} = P^{−1}·u(P) … which reveals that in general the optimal pretraining precision is independent of compute budget."

### 1.8 Eqs. (8)–(10) — δ_PTQ with low-precision training (Sec. 5, lines 1073–1145)
Assumption (line 1073): "We assume training precision is strictly greater than inference precision, and define degradation as identically zero if they are equal."
> δ_PTQ(N, D, P_w, P_post) = C_T · e^{−P_post/γ_post} · (D^{γ_D} / N_eff^{γ_N}) · [1 − e^{−C_w(P_w − P_post)}]      (8)
(first bracket labeled "Overtraining effect", second "Robustification effect"; "C_w is the only new fitted value")
> δ_PTQ(N, D, P_w, P_a, P_kv, P_post) = C_T · e^{−P_post/γ_post} · (D^{γ_D} / N_eff^{γ_N}) · ∏_{x∈{w,a,kv}} [1 − e^{−C_x(P_x − P_post)}]      (9)
> "We measure the fit to the data of such a functional form in Figure 7, and find a strong fit with R² = 0.90 on over 1000 data points (each of 465 pretraining runs post-train quantized to multiple precisions)." (Fig. 7 panel: "MSE: 5.06e-02, R²: 0.9041".)
Eq. (10) (illustrative, C_w = 1, σ²_tr := e^{−P_w/γ_w}): δ_PTQ = C_T (σ²_PTQ − σ²_tr) · D^{γ_D}/N_eff^{γ_N}.

Qualitative claim (lines 1064–1072): two competing effects, "robustification" (training in low precision reduces δ_PTQ) vs "overtraining" (N ↦ N_eff < N raises δ_PTQ); "In practice, the first 'robustification' effect is larger, so that models trained in lower precision overall degrade less when post-train quantized."

### 1.9 Eq. (11) / Finding 4 — unified law (Sec. 5, lines 1146–1157)
> L(N, D, P_w, P_a, P_kv, P_post) = A·N_eff^{−α} + B·D^{−β} + E + δ_PTQ      (11)
> "where δ_PTQ(N, D, P_w, P_a, P_kv, P_post) is in general as in Equation 9 and N_eff(N, P_w, P_a, P_kv) as in Finding 4.2."

### 1.10 Eq. (13) — critical dataset size (App. E.1, lines 1795–1817)
Setting ∂L/∂D = −∂δ_PTQ/∂D with the Sec.-3 forms:
> D_crit = [ β·B·N^{γ_N}·e^{P_post/γ_post} / (γ_D·C_T) ]^{1/(γ_D+β)}      (13)
> "Note that this quantity explodes in P, so that a truly unreasonable amount of data is required for longer pretraining to be harmful at commonly used precisions (eg. 8-bit). However, we find that on overtrained models D/N ≫ 10³, these overtraining-degradation effects become nontrivial around 5-bits, and dominant below that."

Caveats the paper attaches to this table (App. K, lines 2350–2364):
> "We emphasize our numerical constants are unlikely to be useful because as [Hoffmann et al., 2022, Sardana and Frankle, 2023] show, fitted constant depend heavily on the architecture and dataset used … Rather, the trends we identify are the key findings."
> "Note that we include biases in our exponent fits … a fit of the form e^{x/γ_x} in the main text is really computed with offset e^{x/γ_x+n}, but including biases everywhere clutters notation and obscures mathematical insight."

Interpretation notes (mine, not the paper's — the table does not define its symbols): γ_i/n_i are almost certainly the activation ("input") constants γ_a; n_x are the exponent biases; `γ` = 0.5907 is almost certainly γ_post of Eq. 2 and `b` = 1.1277 its bias. C_w, C_a, C_kv of Eqs. 8–9 are **not** reported anywhere in the table. Note γ_D = 0.5068 vs γ_N = 0.3439 — the paper calls these "similar" and treats δ_PTQ as ≈ a power law in D/N; they differ by ~47%.

**Reproduction check (mine):** numerically minimizing Eq. 5 with Table 2 constants under C = (6/16)NDP:
- using the main-text form (1 − e^{−P/γ̄})³ with γ̄ = 1.9475 (mean of γ_w, γ_i, γ_kv) and no biases → P* = 4 bits for all C ∈ [1e18, 1e24];
- using the biased form ∏(1 − e^{−P/γ_x + n_x}) → P* = 7 bits for all C, and 4-bit requires 1/f(4) ≈ 5.2× more parameters (paper: "more than 4x"), 8-bit ≈ 1.21×, 16-bit ≈ 1.01×; f_a(3) < 0 (consistent with the paper not fitting P_a = 3 runs).
So the headline "7-8 bits" is **not reproducible from the main-text formulas plus Table 2**; the omitted biases are load-bearing, and the sign convention of the bias (1 − e^{−P/γ+n}) had to be inferred.

## 2. Experimental setup (Q2)

- **Fit suite (Sec. 2.3, lines 253–259):** "OLMo-style models on the Dolma V1.7 dataset … standard Transformer++ implementation … N ∈ [30, 60, 110, 220] million parameters (non-embedding) and D ∈ [1.5, 3, 6, 13, 26] billion tokens. Our model sizes are relatively small because we train up to a very high D/N ≈ 10³ … over 20 runs at every (N, D): we sweep 8 values of precision for each of the (weights, activations, attention)." Total "465 language models in 3 to 16 bit precisions" (line 132). "We train only two model sizes at 26B due to compute constraints" (Fig. 4 caption, line 603). Training precision sweep P_w, P_a, P_kv ∈ [3, 12] plus BF16 baselines (line 382).
- **Hyperparameters (App. A, lines 1576–1588):** SwiGLU, RoPE, RMSNorm, Adam β = (0.9, 0.95), cosine LR with 10% warmup, peak LR 6e-4 for smallest model scaled by depth-µP, seq len 1024, batch 256, Adam ε 1e-15, weight decay 0.1, extra LayerNorm before projections "following [Ma et al., 2024]". Ablation at seq 512 / batch 128 / wd 1e-3 / LR 1e-4 / 3% warmup on C4 — "We do not fit constants on these ablated runs" (Fig. 8).
- **Quantization during training (Sec. 4, line 384; App. D.1, lines 1725–1737):** "we quantize the forward pass in integer type unless otherwise noted"; "We quantize only the forward pass in this work, to ensure fair comparison between quantization-aware-training (weights only) and low-precision training"; weights per-channel, activations per-tensor (App. I); "We train all our models with fake (simulated) quantization on NVidia H100 GPUs to remain hardware agnostic" (App. D.3, line 1771).
- **PTQ method (Sec. 3, line 267):** "we consider models trained in BF16 and use **GPTQ** [Frantar et al., 2022] to post-train quantize them, replicating our findings with two other methods in Appendix F." App. F (lines 2170–2177): "We replicate on **AWQ** … and **round-to-nearest** quantization … but get slightly different values of fitted constants, as expect[ed]. … The fact they, as well as GPTQ in the main text, share the same failure modes suggests that poor post-training quantization data scaling should be the default expectation for any newly proposed PTQ technique." PTQ is of **weights only** (P_post is "post-train weight precision", line 134); PTQ bit-widths shown INT3–INT6 (Fig. 2).
- **Large-scale validation (Sec. 4.3.2, lines 903–912; App. J):** "compute-matched models at various parameter count and precision ranging from FP4 to FP32 and 220M to 1.6B parameters. We train in floating-point type since that is standard in pretraining … though our scaling laws are fitted on integer type." App. J: "N·P kept constant on 16B tokens". (Abstract says "up to 1.7B parameters"; Sec. 4.3.2 says 1.6B.)
- **FP bit allocations (App. N, line 2752):** "E2M0, E3M0, E4M1, E3M2, E4M2, E5M2, and E5M6 for 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12 bits, respectively, with one sign bit throughout." FP refit of Eq. 3 gives "γ_w = 2.8111 and an exponent bias of b = 0.1240".
- **LR-schedule ablation (App. G, lines 2183–2186):** "we ablate our learning rate and schedule to use warmup with linear decay, as opposed to a cosine schedule … We do so on our 30M model due to compute constraints, finding the degradation with token/parameter ratio persists." Both schedules decay; a constant-LR regime is **not** tested.

## 3. Compute-optimal precision claim and the INT/FP caveat (Q3)

Verbatim, Sec. 4.3.2 (lines 893–912):
> "This suggests that compute-optimal precision should be held fixed to P* while N, D are scaled according to Equation 6. We find this P* to be around 7-8 bits when fitting our scaling law on runs with quantization done to integer type. This has two consequences: first, this means the de-facto practice of training models in 16-bit may be suboptimal. Second, the race to low-precision training may have to stop before going below 4-bits, since this would force model sizes to become disproportionately (more than 4x) larger to maintain loss scaling (see Figure 3, left).
> We test our predictions in Figure 6 at a larger scale. … We train in floating-point type since that is standard in pretraining …, though our scaling laws are fitted on integer type. … We find that scaling fits on integer type are a strong fit until 4-bit precision, at which points the difference between the two types becomes more apparent. The matching of qualitative trends throughout, with the optimum being close to the predicted optimum of P* near 7-8 bits suggests that similar scaling laws may exist across types."

Fig. 6 caption (lines 843–845): "Predictions closely match the empirical trend, but are shifted up by a small amount since floating-point is a more expressive type and will incur lower loss at the same precision."

Finding 3 (verbatim box, lines 927–932): "When N, D, P are optimized jointly, compute-optimal pretraining precision is independent of compute. 16-bit has many unnecessary bits, and 4-bit requires increasing the model size disproportionately to maintain loss scaling. Our fits imply that 7-8 bits are compute-optimal. In contrast, when N is fixed in advance, such as when training a model family on similar data, P*(C) ∝ log C. This suggests that for models that will be significantly overtrained, higher precision during training may be compute-optimal."

Scope disclaimer attached to the whole of Sec. 4.3 (lines 847–850): "the implications of our functional form are true no matter the scale at which future experiments are done, but the numerical values we predict depend on our fitted constants which are fitted on smaller-scale, integer-type experiments."

Footnote 3 (line 946): cost of a forward pass ∝ N·P should be held fixed when comparing; "While N P is the same for both a model of N_0 parameters in 16-bit and one with 2N_0 parameters in 8-bit, the latter has higher N_eff with our γ̄, so will reach a lower pretraining loss on the same data with the same training/inference costs."

## 4. Stated limitations and future work (Q4) — verbatim or close

Sec. 6 "Conclusion and Limitations" (lines 1167–1175):
> "There are limitations to our analysis. First, we use a fixed architecture throughout to examine the effects of precision, parameters, and tokens in a controlled manner. In contrast, low precision training often involves architectural tweaks [Ma et al., 2024, Zhu et al., 2024] that can close much of the gap from a vanilla full precision model. Second, while compute costs do scale linearly with precision, the gains from halving precision are usually less than 2x due to systems overhead. Third, we only consider loss scaling without downstream model evaluations. We emphasize that the trends we find aim to be suggestive rather than prescriptive, and hope future work can more comprehensively examine these effects at larger model scale."

Additional limitations scattered in appendices:
- App. D.1 (lines 1733–1737): "In production pretraining in low precision, the matrix multiplications on the backward pass are also quantized … We leave a detailed analysis of how our observations change when accounting for the backward pass to future work. We use integer quantization throughout to fit our scaling laws for simplicity."
- App. D.2 (lines 1750–1757): "Making a full scaling law for floating-point quantization is more involved than our integer treatment, because the effects of scaling mantissa vs exponent bits are not the same … We leave a commensurately detailed analysis of mantissa vs exponent – and more generally floating point – scaling to future work."
- App. L (lines 2370–2376): "training runs with P_a ≤ 3 or P_kv ≤ 3 are not numerically stable, and often diverge … we find activations are more sensitive, though this could be because we quantize activations per-tensor and weights-per channel … Consequently, we do not fit or validate on runs with activations or attention bits equal to 3. We leave a more detailed analysis of fine-grained sensitivity across layers and types of parameters to future work."
- App. H (line 2187): "**This section is speculative.**" — the only mechanism offered for Finding 1 is (i) sharpness / edge-of-stability growth during training ("we conjecture similar results hold for average case"), (ii) NTK top eigenvalue growth, (iii) hierarchical-learning toy results (staircase polynomials, sparse parity).
- App. K (lines 2353–2356): fitted constants "unlikely to be useful"; biases omitted from main-text formulas.
- App. C / Table 1 (lines 1640–1685): functional form chosen partly for interpretability; held-out validation R² of the chosen N_eff form is **0.82** (3 params) vs 0.84 for "N_eff and D_eff (not tied)" (4 params); "several fitted forms are likely to be reasonable fits to the data."
- App. M (line ~2430): alternative factorizations "may have an equally good fit."
- App. E.3 (lines 2100–2115): inference-cost-only model ignores pretraining cost, so "the absolute values of predicted D* are much larger than would be realistically possible."
- Sec. 2.2 (lines 231–236): "the exact fitted values of all coefficients and exponents can vary drastically based on small implementation differences … we emphasize our contribution is not the numerical values we fit, but the trends and functional forms we identify."

## 6. Implications for the report (`reports/LLM 양자화 연구 아이디어 10선.md`)

Current citations: line 40 (Sec. 0.2 item 3: "정밀도 스케일링 법칙(Kumar et al. [2411.04330] [S], Ouyang et al. [2411.17691] [S])은 적합식이며 기작이 없다") and line 376 (A1: "Kumar/Ouyang의 D^γ 효과는 상당 부분 cosine decay의 말기 곡률 상승"). Both carry the [S] tag.

1. **Upgrade [S] → [V]** for 2411.04330 and cite as **Kumar et al., ICLR 2025 (oral)**.
2. The characterization "적합식이며 기작이 없다" is **correct and can now be quoted**: no theorem exists; App. H "This section is speculative"; App. K "numerical constants are unlikely to be useful". Add that the paper itself concedes alternative functional forms fit nearly as well (Table 1: 0.82 vs 0.84 held-out R²).
3. **Write the exact form** when referring to the "D^γ effect": δ_PTQ = C_T (D^{γ_D}/N^{γ_N}) e^{−P_post/γ_post} with fitted γ_D = 0.5068, γ_N = 0.3439 (Table 2); note the paper approximates it as a power law in D/N although γ_D ≠ γ_N.
4. **State the scale honestly**: fits on N = 30–220M (non-embedding), D = 1.5–26B, D/N up to ~10³, forward-pass-only *simulated* integer quantization, GPTQ weight-only PTQ at INT3–6; validation to 1.6–1.7B in FP. Any extrapolation to 7B+/trillion-token regimes in the report is the report's, not the paper's.
5. **Line 376 (A1 hypothesis about cosine-decay curvature):** two things must be added. (a) Kumar et al. **already conjectured the sharpness / edge-of-stability mechanism** in App. H, so "sharpness" is not a new mechanistic proposal — the novelty can only be the quantitative decomposition and intervention. (b) Kumar et al. **ran an LR-schedule ablation** (App. G, Fig. 12: cosine vs warmup+linear decay, 30M model only) and found the D/N degradation trend persists. This does not refute the Catalan-Tatjer et al. [2510.06213] point (both schedules decay; constant-LR was not tested), but the report should say so explicitly rather than imply Kumar et al. never checked the schedule.
6. **Compute-optimal precision claim**: if the report anywhere repeats "7-8 bits is compute-optimal", attach the paper's own caveats: integer-type fits only; cost model C ∝ NDP ignores systems overhead ("gains from halving precision are usually less than 2x"); FP and INT fits diverge below 4 bits; when N is fixed P* grows ∝ log(D/N); and the 7-8 number depends on exponent biases that are omitted from the printed formulas (my reproduction: without biases P* = 4, with biases P* = 7).
7. **PTQ method**: report should say GPTQ (main) with AWQ and RTN replications, weights only — not "quantization" generically.
8. Useful for the report's KV-cache ideas: the paper's own fitted γ_kv = 0.9578 (smallest γ, i.e., KV cache saturates fastest, but App. M: "KV cache is not sensitive to quantization at higher bit value, but very quickly becomes sensitive around 4-5 bit precision"); activations are the most sensitive and P_a = 3 diverges — but the paper flags this may be a per-tensor granularity artifact (App. I/L).
